Most tools pick a lane: simple for beginners or powerful for experts. Figma refuses to
choose. Its navigation has to work for someone drawing their first rectangle *and* a team
managing thousands of components — in the same UI.

## Keep the surface calm

By default the canvas is quiet: a thin toolbar, collapsible panels, and everything else
tucked away. Beginners aren't overwhelmed because most power is hidden until requested.

<Insight number={1} title="Quiet by default, deep on demand">
  A calm default surface lets novices breathe while experts reach for depth via shortcuts
  and menus.
</Insight>

## Make search the universal shortcut

Figma's quick actions and component search mean you never have to *remember* where
anything lives — you just type what you want. As the project grows, search scales where
menus can't.

<Screenshot
  src="/placeholders/screen-b.svg"
  alt="Figma quick-action search"
  caption="Type-to-find collapses a deep menu tree into a single input."

<PullQuote cite="Figma">
  When a product gets big enough, search stops being a feature and becomes the navigation.
</PullQuote>

## Respect the keyboard

Every meaningful action has a shortcut, and the focus order is deliberate. That serves
power users and accessibility at once — keyboard-first design is rarely just for experts.

<Insight number={2} title="Keyboard-first is accessibility-first">
  Designing for the keyboard speeds up power users and opens the door to people who can't
  use a mouse. The same work pays twice.
</Insight>

## The takeaway

Figma scales by staying calm at rest and deep on demand: hidden power, search as
navigation, and a thorough keyboard model let one interface serve doodlers and design
systems alike.

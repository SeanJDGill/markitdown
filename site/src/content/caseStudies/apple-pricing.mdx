Apple sells premium products at premium prices and rarely competes on cost — yet its
buying flow makes spending more feel like the sensible choice. The persuasion is all in how
the decision is framed.

## Anchor high, then make the upgrade small

The first number you see sets the reference for every other. Lead with a higher tier and a
$100 upgrade reads as trivial; lead with the cheapest and the same $100 feels like a lot.
Apple chooses its anchors with intent.

<Insight number={1} title="The first price frames the rest">
  Anchoring is unavoidable — some number will become the reference point. Decide which one
  it should be.
</Insight>

## Compare on outcomes, not specs

Apple's comparisons translate specs into life: "all-day battery", "pro-level photos". The
user compares experiences they can picture, not gigahertz they can't.

<PullQuote cite="Apple">
  People don't buy specifications. They buy the better version of a moment they already
  care about.
</PullQuote>

## Make the considered choice the default

Pre-selected options, "most popular" labels and a guided buying flow gently route the
hesitant toward the mid-to-high tier — never by force, always by framing.

  src="/placeholders/screen-b.svg"

  caption="A guided flow makes the recommended configuration the path of least resistance."

<Insight number={2} title="Defaults are decisions you make for the user">
  Most people accept the default. Choosing it well — honestly — is one of the highest-
  leverage design choices you have.
</Insight>

## The takeaway

Apple wins the price conversation before it starts: anchor the reference point, compare on
outcomes, and make the considered upgrade the easy default.

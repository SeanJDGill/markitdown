Robinhood took an intimidating, gatekept activity — investing — and made it feel like
tapping through a game. That achievement is real, and so is the controversy: this is a
teardown of frictionless design where friction might have been protective.

## Remove the jargon wall

Traditional brokerages drown newcomers in terminology. Robinhood's onboarding hides almost
all of it, asking plain questions and explaining concepts only when needed. The barrier to
starting collapses.

<Insight number={1} title="Translate the domain, don't test it">
  Don't make users learn your jargon to begin. Meet them in plain language and teach the
  vocabulary as they go.
</Insight>

## Celebrate the first action

The first trade is wrapped in delight — confetti, encouragement, a sense of achievement.
It's brilliant onboarding psychology, and it's exactly where the ethics get hard.

<PullQuote cite="Built for Mars teardown">
  The same delight that helps someone start a good habit can just as easily reward a
  harmful one. Celebration is not neutral.
</PullQuote>

## When friction is a feature

Investing involves real risk, and some friction — confirmations, cooling-off, plain
warnings — protects users from themselves. Robinhood's smoothness is a design triumph that
also raises a duty-of-care question every product handling money or health should ask.

<Screenshot
  src="/placeholders/screen-b.svg"

<Insight number={2} title="Ask who the friction protects">
  Before you remove a step, ask whether it was protecting the user. Frictionless isn't
  automatically ethical.
</Insight>

## The takeaway

Robinhood is a genuine onboarding masterclass and a genuine cautionary tale: the craft that
removes barriers can also remove safeguards. Great UX includes deciding which friction to
keep.

Before Tinder, online dating meant long profiles, search filters and inboxes. Tinder threw
all of it out and replaced it with one gesture. That radical reduction is one of the most
influential interaction design decisions of the last decade.

## Match the interaction to the decision

The swipe is so successful because it fits the judgement perfectly: a fast, gut-level yes
or no. A heavyweight form would have mismatched a lightweight decision; the gesture matches
it exactly.

<Insight number={1} title="Fit the input to the judgement">
  A snap decision deserves a snap interaction. Match the weight of the control to the weight
  of the choice the user is actually making.
</Insight>

## Make the core loop effortless and rewarding

One card, one gesture, repeat — with the occasional match as a variable reward. The loop is
trivial to perform and dopaminergic to continue, which is exactly why it's so sticky.

<PullQuote cite="Tinder">
  The tighter and more rewarding the core loop, the less the rest of the product has to do.
</PullQuote>

## Count the cost of the reduction

Reducing people to a photo and a swipe is efficient and shallow at once. The same
simplification that drove adoption also flattened nuance — a reminder that every reduction
is a trade, not a free win.

<Screenshot
  src="/placeholders/screen-b.svg"

<Insight number={2} title="Every simplification is a trade-off">
  Stripping a decision to its core is powerful, but you lose whatever you stripped away.
  Know what you're trading, and whether it mattered.
</Insight>

## The takeaway

Tinder's swipe is a textbook case of fitting an interaction to a decision — fast, rewarding
and category-defining. It's also a lesson that radical simplicity always has a cost worth
naming.
